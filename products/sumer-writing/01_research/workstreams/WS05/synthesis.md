# Synthesis — WS05

Status: complete

## Answer

The preserved corpus expands from predominantly administrative records and lexical lists in Uruk IV–III to increasingly explicit royal, legal, epistolary, literary, religious and scholarly uses during the third and second millennia. Expansion was uneven: new genres did not replace accounting, and a text’s existence does not prove it governed practice. Human voices are strongest where provenance, genre and translation are secure, but most surviving ‘voices’ remain institutionally filtered.

## Mechanism and chronology

- Uruk IV evidence is overwhelmingly administrative, while lexical texts become substantially more visible in Uruk III.
- By the middle third millennium BCE cuneiform served economic, religious, political, literary and scholarly domains.
- Lexical lists form a continuous knowledge-organizing tradition from the earliest writing through later cuneiform cultures.
- Legal documents, letters and contracts provide human-scale cases but are mediated by scribal formula and institutional context.
- Functional expansion should be narrated as successive additions and recombinations, not a ladder from accounting to literature.

## Strongest evidence

- **Proto-Cuneiform Account-Books and Journals:** pp. 28–31; limitation: Earliest corpus only.
- **The Origins of Writing:** paragraph 69; limitation: Broad chronological compression.
- **DCCLT lexical introduction:** introduction; limitation: Lexical focus.
- **Cuneiform Texts in the Metropolitan Museum, Vol. I:** commercial, legal and letters catalogue; limitation: Collection selection is not representative of total use.

## Contradictions and unknowns

- Continuity involved copying, reordering and transformation, not static preservation.
- First-person grammar is not transparent access to private interiority.
