It’s the high summer of 60 AD and Rome’s rule over Britannia is under dire threat. Governor Gaius Suetonius Paulinas – over the distant jeering of the barbarian hordes – assures his men of their own spirit and that at the sight of this and their weapons, their enemy will break. Riding along her own lines of eager warriors, the Iceni leader Boudica screams her own encouragement: that she – a woman – would fight this day for freedom, her abused daughters, and in answer for her battered body. Will the men do the same or be content with slavery? Roaring their battle-cries as if in answer, the unstoppable mass of barbarian arms and muscle was about to clash with the immovable rock of Rome’s stalwart legions; the battle for Britannia was close at hand. On reviewing a map of the period, dispassionate history buffs may wonder why Rome extended its sway to the far-flung lands of Britain. Always a mediterranean beast, even Augustus advised consolidation over unnecessary expansion. Britannia (or ‘Albion’ to the natives) was hardly a land brimming with riches. Indeed across its centuries-long history as a province of Rome, it usually hosted more legions than far richer provinces like those in Spain, making it an expensive possession. Though Gaius Julius Caesar himself had twice campaigned across the Channel during his Gallic Wars, he made no serious attempt at full conquest. His heir and Rome’s first emperor Augustus also saw the direct rule of the barbarians there as unnecessary. However, unlike these two titans of Roman history, the rather unassuming Claudius was not accomplished as either a great general or master politician at his unexpected rise to the Imperial purple in 41 AD. Claudius was certainly not the ideal image of a mighty Roman emperor. He was forced to assume the purple by the Praetorian Guard. That he survived the unstable tenure of his nephew Caligula, was largely down to his seemingly underwhelming character: Claudius suffered with a stutter, while often needing a slave attendant to recall for him what he had just been saying when his mind drifted; it was even said he tended to drool. Perhaps most importantly for any potential enemies, the ageing Claudius lacked any military experience or prestige and hence there was never an expectation this family embarrassment – who was even disliked by his own mother – would ever ascend as Emperor. Yet despite first appearances, Claudius was no idiot, and ascendent as he was, there was still doubt within the Senate, with many suggesting the restoration of the Republic. Perfectly aware of the boost the subjugation of Brittania would bring, it was against this backdrop that the unlikely conquest was begun in 43 AD, under the leadership of Aulus Plautius. Joining the legions following the initial struggle, Claudius entered Camulodunum in triumph, even accompanied by war elephants, perhaps to the amazement of some of the defeated natives. Back in Rome, his triumphal arch declared that Claudius had taken the submission of eleven native kings who had been overcome with no losses and that he was the first to bring those barbarians across the Ocean under the sway of the Romans. The native peoples the Romans now ruled over existed as a patchwork of tribal kingdoms, not all of whom resisted. As in previous times there were friends of Rome present. Indeed the causus belli of 43 AD was in fact the exile of Verica – the long-reigning king of the Atrebates – who had been driven into the arms of the new emperor by civil strife and the aggression of the Catuvellauni. Another key ally of the invaders was another famous queen – Cartimandua – who ruled the northern Brigantes. It had been this friendly monarch who had handed over the fugitive Caratacus, king of the vanquished Catuvellauni. Another prominent ally in the early years of Roman rule were, of course, the Iceni tribe, a people that dwelt in the region roughly corresponding to modern Norfolk and Suffolk. Unlike their more imprudent cousins, the Iceni king Antedios sought an alliance with the invaders, perhaps cognisant of the inevitability of Roman victory but also as protection from their aggressive western neighbours. Despite a rebellion in 47 around the issue of disarmament, the friendship mostly endured into the reign of Prasutagus, who ruled up to his death in 60 AD. With Roman expansion continuing apace it’s evident that Prasutagus too wisely sought to ease his peoples’ integration into the empire proper by willing his kingdom to both his own daughters but also to the emperor Nero. Unfortunately for his surviving family, the late king didn’t account for Roman greed and brutality. ‘Greed’ perhaps may have struck Roman apologists as harsh. Rome had spent much gold, blood and lives in subduing this far-flung province and it’s likely the procurator Catus Decianus was under particular pressure to extract as much wealth from his barren province as he could. The monetary gifts that had been granted to certain nobles by Claudius were declared as loans that required immediate payment with interest. As Tacitus writes, both the kingdom and household of Prasutagus were then seized as ‘prizes of war,’ the will of the late king violently dismissed. Of course, the widow of Prasutagus, Boudica was understandably indignant and was not quiet about it. In response, the Roman officials turned on the family of the dead king, having Boudica flogged, while her daughters were violated. Beyond this personal humiliation, however, there were other solid reasons to stoke the anger of the Britons. Aside from the heavy tribute and occupation, conscription was an unbearable fact of life. As a friend of Rome, the Iceni and others of their like, were also compelled to submit annual quotas of able-bodied young men for service in the auxiliaries; this reduced the number of potential trouble-makers, but also often sent such men far from home. It’s not hard to imagine, then, why Boudica’s subsequent cry for blood and death was eagerly answered by her people. Soon too, the neighbouring Trinovantes – similarly aggrieved – flocked to her banner, along with other native folk. Though the numbers of fighting men are hard to determine, they likely numbered anywhere from the upper tens of thousands perhaps close to the rather fanciful two hundred and thirty thousand insisted upon by a Roman source. Whatever the true numbers, a great wave of barbarian rage and malice was about to rock Roman Brittania to its very core. The first target would be the hated colonia of Camulodunum, the very site of Claudius’ triumph and even housing a temple dedicated to the late emperor. Boudica was certainly the nominal leader of this vast host, however, to what degree she controlled it to a particular end or agenda is debatable. We know next to nothing of her outside of written Roman accounts. Ironically, though an entirely invented speech prior to battle, Tacitus’ Boudican line: “I am descended from mighty men...” is itself likely accurate, the wife of Prasutagus almost certainly of royal or at least noble rank herself. Aside from this, we can fairly theorise she must have had enough influence and charisma aside from her role as king’s wife to call upon and lead such a great horde of men and followers. In response to the coming storm, the inhabitants of Camulodunum sent word to the procurator of Roman Britain, Catus Decianus for aid. His response of sending just a couple of hundred poorly armed slaves to assist must have baffled and angered the veterans in equal measure. Though formerly a fortress, Camulodunum had shed its defences, the only defensible structure being the great temple of Claudius itself. Meanwhile, a serious response had been attempted by soldiers of Legio VIIII Hispana. Led by future governor Quintus Petillius Cerialis, these two thousand men were ambushed en route and were mostly wiped out; Cerialis himself did manage to escape, however. Back at Camulodunum, Boudica’s warriors swarmed into the settlement, mercilessly hacking their way towards the temple itself. Man, woman and child were not spared; we can only imagine the grim anticipation of the sword, the last sound heard by many a Roman being the shrill screech of the barbarian screamer. Others were less fortunate, crucified or hung, with some perishing in the flames that would engulf the whole settlement. The first barbarian wave overran the place, while a second attack was focused on the large and hated temple of Claudius. Though likely putting up a decent resistance over two days, the veterans held up there stood little chance against the sheer numbers and ferocity of Boudica’s warriors. Having smashed the last fragments of resistance, Camulodunum was burnt to the ground, with the destruction being so complete as to leave what is known today as the ‘Boudiccan destruction horizon’ – a distinct layer of earth easily identifiable by modern archaeologists. Back west, far from the epicenter of Boudica’s massive revolt, the fifth governor of Britannia, Gaius Seutonius Paulinus was dealing with another perceived threat in the druids of Mona. He was stuck in northern Wales that was sparsely populated, and heavily wooded, with few navigable tracks. His target was the isle of Mona, a flat, fertile, and populated area. Most importantly, it was a source of copper. Given its status as the effective breadbasket of northern Wales, this fact alone made it an obvious avenue of control. Perhaps as immediately important for the Romans, the druids – the effective intellectual and priestly class of their time – were also perceived as a natural focus of resistance and had to be dealt with. The governor had already campaigned in the difficult territory for two seasons, and by the third had isolated the holy isle. He likely crossed at the narrowest place – corresponding to the modern-day Menai Straight – using flat-bottomed boats in the main, while his cavalrymen used fordable points, with many swimming across alongside their steeds. With Paulinas were the legions XIIII Gemina and XX, with the support of an undesignated number of auxiliaries, including the renowned Batavi cohorts, famed for their amphibious capabilities and fierceness. Once ashore, the superstitious Romans were confronted by armed resistance, yes, but also the terrifying spectacle of crazed and wild women, adorned in black robes and chanting ominously, while wielding smouldering firebrands. Undoubtedly riling up their men for the clash, they screamed and cursed at the Roman approach. In addition, many Druids too – arms flailing and spitting out curses – froze many a Roman to the spot in fear of this unknown magic. Alas for the barbarian defenders, the spell would be broken with the harsh rebukes of Paulinas himself. Paulinas’ soldiers fought their way on, cleaving a path through the gathered host. Those defenders not cut down perished in an inferno of their own making and with this gory conclusion, a way of life was definitively destroyed. Following the battle, the sacred groves were thrown down and the druids suppressed. Around the same time that the last embers of the Druid’s army were dying, a fast messenger reached Paulinas to inform him of the far greater threat to the east. With Camulodunum desolated, he left the army in Wales behind and rode with all haste towards the next barbarian target. Boudica’s horde focused their wrath on the new Roman town of Londinium. With his army still too far west to defend the town, Paulinus was forced to leave its inhabitants to their grizzly fate. Perhaps many folk followed his example, heading in the direction of the approaching Roman forces. For Paulinas, the salvation of the province would better be assured by his reuniting with his army. The ominous Boudican destruction horizon scars the modern City’s underbelly too, with Boudica’s horde stripping the town bare and leaving a trail of bloody destruction and depredation in its wake. Lumbering along what would become Watling Street, the Britons set their sights on Verulamium as the next stop on their path of destruction. Like Camulodunum before it, Verulamium – corresponding to modern St Albans – was a town built over existing tribal centres. Verulamium was significant as the old capital of the Catuvellauni, the tribe of the enemy of Caesar, Cassivellaunus. Evidently, word appears to have travelled fast as though here too the ‘Boudiccan destruction horizon’ is present, there is also a conspicuous absence of caches of coins, indicating that the inhabitants had time to remove themselves and their movable goods away from the horde. The precise location of the battle will likely never be known, though the site of Mancetter is a decent candidate. The rapid movement and deployment of such a large number of men still required time and then even more so on deciding on the field of battle and to give Paulinas’ men time to rest up. The governor also likely hesitated to meet Boudica’s horde too far down Watling Street, given he had also dispatched orders for Legio II Augusta to reinforce him. In the event, Poenius Postumus – the effective commander of the legion – failed to respond, depriving his comrades of much needed support. Paulinas, meanwhile, positioned the army he did have – numbering around eleven thousand men – in what Tacitus describes as a defile, his flanks and rear guarded by wooded slopes that necessarily funnelled the more numerous barbarian hoard into a frontal assault. Though Boudica and her lieutenants may not have known it, this was a daunting challenge even for their vast numbers. Indeed, though counter-intuitive to us, the Roman commander may have welcomed a pitched battle over the dangerous prospect of a prolonged guerilla war. In a set-piece battle the Roman had the clear advantage, whereas the barbarian ambush of the ninth legion had shown just how deadly they could be in this style of conflict. For their part, Boudica’s horde of at least tens of thousands of warriors likely felt confidence in their numbers, the boldest among them eagerly awaiting the first blood of battle. This is easy to understand; perhaps even Boudica too – buoyed up by previous easy victories – dared to hope that through sheer ferocity and force of numbers her valiant countrymen could expel the Romans. Barbarian scouts on the day would have spied the Romans arrayed as followed: with the legions dead centre, the auxiliary cohorts flanking these, and the alae completing the formation. The stark and silent line of scutum shields were the obvious and only target for the frenzied and screaming noble charioteers and the masses who would follow them. Before battle commenced, however, the Iceni leader likely rode herself along the lines of braying warriors, screaming her encouragement, perhaps also warning of their grim fate should the Romans emerge victorious and with her injured daughters at her side. Though the exact words of Tacitus are almost certainly fictitious, perhaps he captured some of the sentiment of Boudica’s actual mood. Proclaiming loudly that she was descended from mighty men, for her part she – a woman – would fight not for riches or power but on account of her bruised body, her lost freedom and her outraged daughters. Urging her followers to consider how and why they fight, she closed with a barbed encouragement to the proud warriors present that they could submit to servitude if they will, while she would fight. Perhaps quietly fingering their pila and weapons, the Romans too were assured by the words of their leader. Paulinas dismissed their foes as ill-equipped savages. Directing them to stick to their bloody work before booty is thought of, each man would mercilessly batter their enemies with the boss of their shields before swiftly plunging their short swords into the exposed flesh of their foes. Through such butchery they would have both their plunder and the victory. The contrast between the two armies must have been stark. The opening of hostilities saw the noble-occupied chariots driving furiously to and thro, like an angry swarm of hornets, the racket of the ridings interspersed with the occasional cry of a struck Roman and the curses and insults of the charioteers. Grim-looking, the Roman shields must have presented a daunting wall of silence, the steely discipline of these men little fazed by the racket. Though always a notable spectacle to the Roman mind, these chariots were not meant as some unanswerable bludgeon to break the formations of enemies alone and following on from these came the masses of freemen on foot. Armed in the main with a short spear or long sword and shield, the vast majority of Boudica’s men would have been freemen farmers; they were hardy men toughened by toil and hardship, though compared to their Roman adversaries they were poorly-equipped though possessing a fierce courage – enabled by the presence of their peers – they were undoubtedly ill-trained (if at all). Such men naturally formed into groups along familial, tribal or clan lines, with this – along with the presence of large numbers of women, children and kin in the vast half-ring of wagons to the rear – being a strong factor urging a reckless daring in these desperate men. However, bravery alone would not avail them. The geography of the field now funnelled the hoard into a crushed mass; the upward slope would have also sapped much of the charge’s momentum. Upon almost reaching fighting distance the enemy front line discharged their pila, further dampening the attack; likely killing many, while also forcing others to discard their shields, the devastation of the volleys was compounded with the counter-charge of Paulinas’ men. Advancing steadily but inexorably, the prophecy of Paulinus’ earlier words was fulfilled, his men battering the faces and bodies of the oncoming Britons then thrusting hard with their short swords. When a barbarian fell, he was mercilessly trampled, the Roman steamroller solid and sure. Tacitus writes that the matter was settled quickly, however, given the numbers involved and the undoubted courage and increasingly desperate plight of the men and their families in the rear, it’s more likely the account of Cassius Dio is closer to the truth. The Romans advanced steadily but slowly, at least initially, the sheer crush of barbarian steel and muscle a truly horrid spectacle to those distraught spectators in the rear. Wielding long swords and spears, the mass of Britons likely had small room to effectively use these weapons, while the short swords of the Romans carved a bloody path towards the rough semi-circle of wagons to the rear. Advancing also in wedge formations, this too had the deadly effect of channelling the masses of trapped Britons further into localised killing zones. Upon finally reaching the wagons, many tens of thousands of Britons had either fallen to Roman blades or been trampled underfoot. The general pandemonium was worsened by the presence of the wagons that now acted as a broad bar to escape. Yet Paulinus’ soldiers harboured little mercy, slaughtering man, woman, child and beast. Given it was likely high summer, even the lingering daylight conspired to increase the native death toll. When the slaughter died down, and the cries for mercy or a swift death were dampened, the total death toll was around eighty thousand, with Tacitus writing the Romans lost just four hundred. Even bearing in mind typical Roman hyperbole in order to glorify the triumph there was little doubt Boudica’s rebellion had been decisively crushed. The Iceni leader herself was among the few to escape the carnage, though she supposedly took her own life shortly afterwards. Cassius Dio reports differently, that she died of illness, but the result was all the same. The barbarian horde was destroyed, and its leader was dead. Similarly to Boudica, the feckless Poenius Postumus – having disobeyed his commander’s call and denied his legion’s share in the glory – fell on his sword. The legion itself was disgraced, remaining the II Augusta forever after. Undoubtedly, if Paulinas and his men had suffered the same fate as the ninth legion, Rome may have abandoned the province completely; indeed Nero considered the possibility and evidently the procurator Catus Decianus clearly felt the same given his own flight as the rebellion grew. Yet victory belonged to the Romans and with significant reinforcements from the continent Paulinas set about punishing those centres of resistance now left open to attack. As promised the remaining Iceni and Trinovantes howled as an avenging path of fire and blood was carved through their lands. To make an already apocalyptic situation worse, the swell of violence and plunder had been so sudden and focused that little work had been done in the fields over the previous months, ensuring a biting famine. Throw into the mix the huge loss of manpower in the dead, crippled, enslaved or fugitives and the future was bleak indeed for the native vanquished. In any event, Paulinas would not long remain governor of the province. In 61 AD he was withdrawn and replaced by the less warlike Petronius Turpilianus. The following years would see a period of quiet consolidation until the advent of the Flavian dynasty and the resumption of expansion, leading the legionary eagles to the very of edge of the Roman world itself. Here, another famous general of the island’s long history would face the fierce prospect of the Caledonian horde, but that is a story for another time.
