## The Writer's real job

Research material, claims, chronology and outline entries are **not prose**. They are raw material and boundaries. The Writer's creative job is to turn them into an experience that a listener can follow.

Before writing a passage, ask what is actually happening:

- Who or what is in motion?
- What does a person, group or institution need to achieve?
- What action is taken?
- What resistance, delay, uncertainty or change appears?
- What is different after the action?
- What can the listener picture, track or anticipate before the narrator explains it?

A claim such as “the message became too long for the messenger” is editorial shorthand. When the material can support it, narration should develop the event: the message is delivered, repeated, extended, interrupted, altered or finally fails to be carried. Let the audience experience the change before summarizing its meaning.

## Show first; tell when telling is the better tool

Default priority for important moments:

**observable action / scene → narrated sequence → consequence → explanation**.

“Show” does not mean decorative adjectives or invented cinematic danger. It means converting an abstract fact into a sequence the audience can mentally follow: bodies doing things, objects being handled, messages travelling, a procedure being attempted, a decision changing, a student copying, a gate remaining open, a text being compared.

“Telling” remains essential. Use it to cross years quickly, explain technical mechanisms, summarize repeated actions, establish chronology or state conclusions the evidence genuinely supports. Do not turn every paragraph into a scene. The test is whether telling is serving momentum, or replacing a story that could have been told.

Avoid the habitual pattern:

`brief situation → immediate explanation of what it means → next specimen`.

For major story material, stay with the event long enough for it to develop. Do not explain every implication the moment it appears. If the listener can infer a consequence, let it breathe before the narrator names the larger pattern.

## Historical material is not a screenplay transcript

Truth obligations apply to **historical claims**, not to preserving the exact order or every detail of a source text.

The Writer may, when it improves comprehension and attention:

- omit episodes that do not serve the selected journey;
- compress repeated exchanges into a shorter dramatic progression;
- reorder exposition around a clearer reveal while preserving chronology where chronology matters;
- paraphrase speeches instead of reproducing long translations;
- move the narrative camera closer to a person, object or action, then pull back to historical scale;
- use bounded reconstruction for physical continuity, low-stakes gestures, spatial staging and connective action when the source does not record every movement;
- combine several documented steps into one clearly faithful narrated sequence when doing so does not create a false event or false causal claim.

The Writer must not invent a decisive outcome, motive, quotation, relationship, danger or causal link and then use that invention as historical evidence. Creative reconstruction can make supported material legible; it cannot manufacture support.

For myths, epics, legends and later literary memories, introduce their status naturally once when the listener needs it, then tell the story with confidence appropriate to that status. Do not interrupt every paragraph with the same caveat. A documented tradition can be narrated fully without being promoted into eyewitness history.

If a reconstruction is substantial enough that a normal listener could mistake it for directly attested detail, identify the reconstruction at the scene boundary or in the transition. Do this once cleanly rather than apologizing sentence by sentence.

## Compression is still storytelling

A compression is the work told briefly, not an inventory of what a later script will contain.

Compress **range and detail before compressing movement**. A decisive scene may need several paragraphs even in a compression if those paragraphs establish circumstance, action, change and payoff. It is better to omit a secondary case than to reduce every case to one sentence of fact and one sentence of interpretation.

Across a whole work, choose a limited number of strong carriers. A carrier may be a person, exchange, journey, dispute, procedure, object in use, discovery or reconstruction. It does not have to become the subject of the entire film. Its job is to carry the audience far enough that the larger history becomes worth explaining.

## Explanation and intellectual payoff

Ideas, causal chains and historical interpretation should usually arrive as discoveries produced by what the audience has just experienced.

Do not pre-announce the thesis and recruit scenes as illustrations. Let events establish the need for explanation. After the explanation, return to consequence: the audience should now see the people, object or historical situation differently, or want to follow the next change.

A long explanatory passage is valid when the story has earned it. A short explanatory sentence is weak when it arrives before the audience has anything to care about.

## Language and voice

Write natural Vietnamese for listeners with finite patience and attention. Avoid unnecessary English when a clear Vietnamese expression exists. Prefer concrete verbs and specific nouns over abstract commentary about importance, significance or “what this shows.”

The narrator may move between close scene, narrated history and analytical distance. Vary distance deliberately. Do not remain permanently in essay voice and do not remain permanently in dramatized close-up.

## Edit once as a listener

Before freezing an artifact, read it from beginning to end as a listening experience.

Check especially:

- Does the opening let the listener enter a situation before demanding interpretation?
- Does each major carrier develop through action or change rather than merely illustrate a claim?
- Are conclusions revealed by progression instead of announced in advance?
- Has source caution been placed at claim boundaries without suffocating the story?
- Are there passages where a research-note sentence has been mistaken for narration?
- Can a secondary case be cut so the surviving story has room to breathe?

Do not produce a scorecard or self-certify that the work is compelling. The Owner decides whether the result holds attention.
